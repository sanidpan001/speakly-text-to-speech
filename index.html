Text to Speech, Explained: How Speakly Reads Anything Aloud for Free

Text to speech — often shortened to TTS — is the technology that converts written words into spoken audio. What began in the 1960s as robotic, barely intelligible experiments has become one of the most quietly useful tools on the modern web. Today, every major operating system ships with a capable speech engine built in, and Speakly puts that engine on a stage: paste any text, choose a voice, adjust the pace and tone, press play, and listen while the current word lights up on screen. This guide explains how text to speech works, who benefits from it, how to get the most natural sound from it, and why a follow-along word highlight makes such a difference to comprehension.

How text to speech actually works

When you press play in Speakly, your browser hands the text to the speech synthesizer built into your device — the same engine behind screen readers and voice assistants. That engine first normalizes the text: it expands numbers, abbreviations, and symbols into speakable words, so "Dr. Smith arrived at 5 p.m." becomes something a voice can actually pronounce. Next it breaks the sentence into phonemes, the smallest units of sound in a language, and plans the prosody — the rhythm, stress, and intonation that make speech sound human rather than mechanical. Finally, the voice itself renders those sounds. Older voices stitched together tiny recorded fragments; modern neural voices, like the ones included in recent versions of Chrome, Edge, macOS, and Android, generate the waveform with machine-learning models trained on hours of real narration. That is why a voice on a new phone can sound startlingly close to a person reading in the room with you.

Because Speakly runs entirely in your browser through the Web Speech API, nothing you paste is uploaded anywhere. The voices available in the picker are exactly the voices installed on your device, which is why the list differs between a Windows laptop, an iPhone, and an Android tablet. Some browsers also offer network voices that stream from the cloud for higher quality; your device marks those in its own voice list. Either way, there is no account, no file conversion queue, and no waiting — synthesis starts the moment you press play.

Why listening beats re-reading more often than you think

Reading is a visual skill, but comprehension is not purely visual. Listening engages a different channel, and for many tasks it is simply the better channel. Students use text to speech to proofread essays, because errors that the eye skips — a doubled word, a missing "not," an awkward sentence that runs too long — become obvious when heard aloud. Language learners listen to correct pronunciation while following the highlighted text, binding sound to spelling far faster than either activity alone. Busy professionals turn long reports, documentation, and newsletters into something they can absorb on a walk, in the kitchen, or during a commute, reclaiming hours that would otherwise be lost to a screen.

Accessibility is the deepest reason of all. For readers with dyslexia, low vision, ADHD, or other print disabilities, hearing text while watching each word highlight can transform reading from a struggle into a routine. Research on bimodal reading — presenting text visually and audibly at the same time — consistently shows improved word recognition and retention for developing readers. Speakly's follow-along reader was designed with exactly this in mind: the spoken word glows amber on the page, finished words soften, and upcoming words stay quiet, so your eyes always know where your ears are.

The voice picker: finding a voice you actually like

Not all synthetic voices are equal, and the single biggest upgrade to your listening experience is choosing a good one. Most devices include at least one high-quality voice per language, and many include dozens. In Speakly, the voice picker lists every voice your system offers, and the language filter narrows the list instantly — useful when your device ships with forty voices but you only ever read in two languages. As a rule, voices labelled "natural," "neural," "premium," or "enhanced" sound noticeably smoother than the compact defaults installed for size reasons. If you read in multiple languages, match the voice to the text's language: an English voice reading Spanish will pronounce every word as if it were English, which is intelligible but rarely pleasant, while a Spanish voice will roll through the same paragraph beautifully.

Gender, age, and accent are matters of taste, and they genuinely affect stamina. A voice you find grating at minute one will be unbearable at minute forty, so it is worth spending a moment sampling two or three candidates on a paragraph you know well. Many listeners prefer a slightly lower-pitched voice for long nonfiction and a brighter voice for dialogue-heavy fiction. Speakly remembers nothing between visits — your text stays yours — but your browser typically keeps your chosen system defaults, so the picker tends to start near a voice you already trust.

Rate and pitch: the two sliders that change everything

Speaking rate is measured here as a multiplier of the voice's natural pace. At 1×, most voices read at roughly 160 to 175 words per minute, close to comfortable conversational speed and ideal for dense or technical material. Dropping to 0.75× slows the narration enough to study, transcribe, or learn pronunciation — language learners often go slower still. Raising the rate to 1.5× or beyond is the listener's equivalent of skimming: ideal for news, email triage, and re-reading familiar material, though comprehension of new, complex ideas falls off quickly above about 1.75×. Speakly's presets — Study, Normal, and Skim — cover the three speeds most people actually use, and the estimated listening time updates live as you drag, so you always know whether that article fits into your walk.

Pitch shifts the voice up or down without changing its speed. Small adjustments, a tenth or two either way, can rescue a voice that sounds almost right: slightly lower pitch often adds authority and calm for presentations and study notes, while slightly higher pitch adds energy that suits children's stories and dialogue. Extreme pitch values sound cartoonish on most voices — entertaining for a chapter of a comic novel, less so for a legal contract. Because pitch interacts with each voice's design, treat the slider as seasoning: adjust, listen to one sentence, and adjust again.

Long texts and the chunking trick

Every speech engine has limits. Hand a browser a 10,000-word chapter in a single request and some engines stall, skip, or stop mid-sentence — a well-known quirk that has frustrated listeners for years. Speakly solves this with long-text chunking. Before playback begins, your text is split into short, self-contained passages of about 180 characters, cut at sentence boundaries wherever possible and at commas or word boundaries when a single sentence runs long. The player then speaks the chunks back-to-back like tracks on an album, and the progress bar counts words across the whole piece, so a forty-minute listen flows without babysitting. Chunking also makes pausing and restarting precise: stop anywhere, press play, and Speakly resumes cleanly from a sensible boundary instead of garbling a half-finished sentence.

This design means there is no practical length limit. Paste an entire research paper, a full chapter of a public-domain novel, your complete lecture notes, or a long email thread — the stats line tells you how many chunks it became and how long the full listen will take at your chosen speed. If the estimate says twelve minutes, you genuinely have a twelve-minute audio companion, generated fresh on your device, for free.

Practical ways people use Speakly every day

Writers and editors use it as a ruthless proofreader, hearing clunky sentences the eye forgives. Students convert lecture slides and textbook sections into audio for revision walks, slowing difficult passages and speeding through review. Teachers play passages aloud in class while students follow the highlight, modelling fluent reading. Content creators draft scripts and listen back to check timing and tone before recording. Job seekers rehearse interview answers by hearing their written responses spoken in a neutral voice. Accessibility users make it their default reader for everything from news to bank letters. And countless listeners simply prefer their long reads as audio: research digests with morning coffee, documentation during chores, newsletters on the train.

To get the best results, prepare your text lightly. Remove navigation menus, cookie notices, and page clutter copied along with an article, since the voice will faithfully read all of it. Break giant walls of text with paragraph spacing, spell out unusual abbreviations the first time, and write numbers the way you want them heard if the automatic expansion guesses wrong. Then choose your voice, set a pace that matches the material, and let the highlight carry your attention. Listening is the oldest way humans have taken in stories and knowledge; Speakly simply gives that ancient channel a play button.

Frequently asked questions

Is Speakly really free, with no limits?

Yes. Speakly is completely free with unlimited usage — no signup, no word caps, no paywall, and no watermark. It works by using the text-to-speech voices already built into your browser and device, so there is no server cost to pass on to you and nothing to unlock.

Which voices can I choose from?

The voice picker lists every speech voice installed on your device, across all languages, and the language filter helps you narrow the list. The exact voices depend on your system: Windows, macOS, iOS, Android, and Chrome all ship different sets, and browsers like Edge and Chrome add their own natural-sounding options.

How does the word-by-word highlighting work?

Speakly builds a follow-along reader from your text and listens for word-boundary signals from the speech engine as it talks. The word currently being spoken glows amber, words already read fade softly, and the reader scrolls to keep pace. On the rare engine that does not send boundary signals, Speakly falls back to a timing estimate based on your chosen rate so the highlight keeps moving.

Can Speakly read very long articles or whole chapters?

Yes — that is what its long-text chunking is for. Before playback, your text is split into short passages at sentence boundaries, and the player speaks them seamlessly one after another. This avoids the stalls and cut-offs some speech engines hit with very long single requests, so there is no practical length limit.

Is my text private when I use Speakly?

Yes. Speakly runs entirely in your browser and never uploads, stores, or records your text. Speech is generated by your own device's built-in engine. Some browsers offer optional network voices that stream text to their provider for higher quality; if you pick a local voice, everything stays on your device.

What do the rate and pitch sliders do?

Rate controls speaking speed from half speed (0.5×) for study and dictation up to double speed (2×) for skimming, with presets for study, normal listening, and skimming. Pitch raises or lowers the voice's tone without changing speed — slightly lower sounds calmer, slightly higher sounds brighter. The estimated listening time updates as you adjust the rate.
